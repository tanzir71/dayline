Many autistic children do not say "it hurts", or show pain in ways that are easy to read as behaviour. A sudden change in your child is a good reason to think about pain first.

## 1. Signs that can mean pain

- A sudden change: more hard moments, new hitting or head-banging, crying that does not settle, or becoming very quiet.
- Touching, pressing, rubbing or hitting one part of the body, like an ear, the jaw, the head or the tummy.
- Sleep changes: waking often, not wanting to lie down, or wanting to sleep sitting up.
- Eating changes: refusing food, chewing on one side, avoiding hot or cold food, or chewing on things more.
- Moving differently: limping, not using one arm, curling up, or not letting you touch one place.

## 2. Check gently

- Look at the usual places: teeth and gums, ears, throat, tummy, skin (rashes, bites, cuts), feet and shoes, and tight clothes.
- Think about the toilet: constipation, pain when peeing, or a change in poo.
- Use a body picture or a doll and ask your child to point. Some children can hold up fingers to show how much it hurts.
- Note what you see in Dayline: when it started, and what makes it better or worse. It helps the doctor.

## 3. Comfort while you find out

- Lower the noise and light, and keep things calm and familiar.
- Offer comfort the way your child likes it, without forcing.
- Ask your child's doctor before giving any pain medicine. Dayline never suggests medicines or doses.

## See a doctor promptly if

- The change is sudden and does not settle, or your child keeps holding one part of the body.
- There is fever, vomiting, a swollen tummy, blood in poo or pee, or your child will not walk or use an arm or leg.
- Your child has had a fall or a knock to the head, or may have swallowed something.
- Danger signs, like trouble breathing, being floppy or very hard to wake, a seizure, or very severe pain: go to a hospital emergency department or call 999.
