Constipation is common in autistic children and easy to miss. Many children do not say "my tummy hurts", so it can show up as changes in behaviour, sleep or eating instead.

## 1. Signs to watch for

- Fewer poos than usual for your child, or hard, dry, pellet-like poo.
- Straining, crying or hiding to poo, or holding it in by crossing legs, standing on tiptoe or stiffening.
- A tummy that looks swollen or feels hard, more wind, or a smaller appetite.
- Soiling, or runny poo leaking into pants. This can happen when hard poo is stuck higher up, so it is not always diarrhoea.
- More hard moments, poor sleep, pressing the tummy against furniture, or not wanting to sit.

## 2. What you can do today

- Keep a simple note for two weeks: the days your child poos, what it looks like, and any pain. Doctors find this very useful.
- Offer water often through the day, in the cup or bottle your child likes.
- Offer foods with fibre that your child already accepts, like fruit or vegetables, without pressure.
- Give a calm, regular toilet time, for example after breakfast. Put a small stool under their feet, and keep the bathroom bright and unhurried.
- Encourage movement: walking, climbing, playing outside.

## 3. Talking to the doctor

- If constipation lasts more than a week or two, or keeps coming back, see your child's doctor.
- Bring your notes, and say how your child usually shows pain.
- Ask before giving any laxative, home remedy or other product. Dayline never suggests medicines or doses; ask the doctor.

## See a doctor promptly if

- There is blood in the poo, severe tummy pain, or vomiting.
- Your child has a fever with tummy pain, or a swollen, hard tummy.
- Your child has not pooed for many days and seems unwell, or is losing weight.
- Danger signs, like very severe pain, green vomit, or your child being floppy or very hard to wake: go to a hospital emergency department or call 999.
