Many autistic children wake in the night, sometimes for hours; it is exhausting, and it is not your fault. Looking for what wakes your child, and keeping night-time calm and dull, can help.

## 1. Look for what wakes your child

- Keep a short note for two weeks: when your child wakes, for how long, and what happened that day (food, naps, outings, changes, illness).
- Check the room at night: too hot or cold, mosquitoes, light from the street, noise, a scratchy blanket or clothes.
- Think about the body: a full bladder, hunger, thirst, constipation, itching, or tooth or ear pain. Pain is easy to miss if your child does not say it.
- Look at the day: a long late nap, little time outdoors, or screens near bedtime can push sleep later.

## 2. At bedtime

- Keep the same short bedtime steps every night.
- Toilet and a small drink before lights out.
- Set the room up for the whole night: a fan, a mosquito net, a dim night light if your child likes one, a favourite object within reach.
- Where you can, let your child fall asleep the way things will be when they wake at 2 am. If you are there at the start, they may look for you in the night.

## 3. When your child wakes

- Keep it calm and dull: low light, few words, a soft voice. Use the same short phrase each time, such as "It is night. Time to sleep."
- Check for a need: toilet, water, too hot, pain. Meet it quietly.
- Avoid screens, bright lights and play. They tell the body it is daytime.
- If your child is wide awake for a long time, a quiet activity in dim light until they are sleepy can work better than a struggle.

## When to get help

- If night waking goes on for weeks, or the whole family is worn out, talk to your child's doctor. Bring your two-week notes.
- Tell the doctor soon about loud snoring, pauses in breathing, or waking in pain.
- Ask the doctor before giving any medicine or product for sleep.
