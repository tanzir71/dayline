Bangladesh has laws that protect the rights of autistic children and adults, including the right to go to school and to get support. This is a plain-language overview, not legal advice; for how things work in your area, ask the offices named here.

## 1. The laws

- The Rights and Protection of Persons with Disabilities Act 2013 names autism as a type of disability. It sets out rights such as education, health care, work and taking part in community life, and says people with disabilities must not face discrimination.
- The Neurodevelopmental Disability Protection Trust Act 2013 set up a trust to support people with neurodevelopmental disabilities, including autism, and their families.
- You do not need a lawyer to ask about these rights. Knowing the names of the laws helps when you talk to schools and offices.

## 2. School

- The law supports inclusive education: children with disabilities have the right to study in mainstream schools alongside other children.
- Ask the school what changes they can make: a quiet place to go, extra time, a seat near the door, a support person, or a shorter day at first.
- Put requests in writing and keep a copy. Bring your child's brief and any assessment reports to meetings.
- If a school turns your child away because of disability, ask your local education office and the Upazila social services office for help.

## 3. ID card and allowance

- You can apply through the Department of Social Services to have your child identified and registered as a person with a disability. Registered people get a disability identity card, often called the Suborno Nagorik card.
- The card can help when you apply for support, such as the disability allowance paid through the Department of Social Services.
- Who is eligible, which papers you need and how to apply can change. Ask at your Upazila social services office (in a city, your area's social services office).

## 4. Getting ready

- Keep one folder with copies of the birth registration certificate, assessment reports, prescriptions and school papers.
- Write your questions down before you go. Note who you spoke to, when, and what they said.
- Take someone with you if you can. Parent groups often know how things work locally.

## When to get help

- If your child is refused a service or treated unfairly, ask the Upazila social services office or a disability organisation for advice.
- For legal questions, talk to a lawyer or a legal aid service.
- If your child is in danger, call 999.
