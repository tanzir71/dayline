Many autistic children eat a short list of foods, often because of taste, texture, smell, colour or how food looks, not because they are being difficult. Pressure usually makes it harder; calm, small steps help more.

## 1. Set up calm meals

- Keep meals at regular times, in the same seat, with the same plate or cup if that helps.
- Put at least one food your child already eats on the plate every time.
- Keep meals short and calm. Turn the TV down. Eat together when you can.
- Let your child serve themselves, or keep foods apart on the plate if touching foods bother them.

## 2. Try new foods slowly

- Put a new food near your child, on the table or on a side plate. Looking at it counts.
- Start close to what your child likes: another brand of the same biscuit, a little dal beside the rice, a fruit with the same crunch.
- Let your child touch, smell, lick or help cook the food without having to eat it.
- Offer the same new food many times over weeks. It can take a long time before a child tries it.
- Skip bribes, hiding foods inside other foods, and forcing. They can make meals feel less safe.

## 3. Keep notes, not worries

- Note everything your child eats in a week, including snacks and drinks. The list is often longer than it feels.
- Share the list with family so everyone offers the same safe foods.
- Notice what helps: a certain plate, a time of day, being hungry, a calm room.

## When to get help

- Talk to your child's doctor if your child is losing weight, not growing, very tired, often constipated, or the list of foods is getting shorter.
- Also tell the doctor if eating seems hard or painful, such as gagging, coughing or choking on food.
- Ask the doctor before giving any supplement, vitamin or special diet. Be wary of diets or products that claim to change or cure autism.
